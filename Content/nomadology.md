## SECTION 1: The Brief

Design a service for campers that improves
their overall experience in terms of safety,
accommodation, sanitation, and food.

Four clear problem areas.

But as we moved into research, a different
question started to surface:

Why do these same problems appear across
every campsite, every location,
with every provider?

That question shifted the direction
of the project entirely.

---

## SECTION 2: Meet Izza (Primary Persona)

Age: 23 · Student · Mumbai
Archetype: The explorer

She isn't asking for luxury.

She wants to experience the rawness of nature
with her close ones — and feel safe and human
while doing it.

After every camping trip, three things let her down:
dirty washrooms, poor food, and uncomfortable sleep.

She isn't asking for a hotel in a forest.
She just wants the basics to work.

---

## SECTION 3: The Context

Camping is growing rapidly:
- Market growth at 6.5% CAGR from 2021 to 2025
- Urban life is pushing people to seek nature more
- Camping is no longer occasional — it's intentional

But the experience hasn't kept up:
- Safety remains a concern
- Hygiene is inconsistent
- The equipment market has evolved
  but the on-ground experience has not

The gap wasn't demand.
It was how the experience was being delivered.

---

## SECTION 4: Research Approach

We studied both:
1. How people experience camping
2. How camping services actually operate

### Interviews — campers & providers
Uncovered the gap between expectations and
reality from both sides of the service.

### Focus groups — campers & non-campers
Surfaced motivations, fears, and the
difference between those who camp and
those who want to but haven't.

### Photo journaling — real campsite conditions
This revealed something interviews couldn't.
Not what people said about camping —
but what they valued without articulating.

A campfire surrounded by friends.
A simple meal shared outdoors.
A tent opening into a forest.

These moments pointed to something deeper:
People weren't chasing comfort.
They were chasing rawness and connection.

### Market scan — equipment & service landscape
Helped us understand what already existed,
and where the service gap actually began.

---

## SECTION 5: The Key Finding

At first, the issues seemed disconnected:
hygiene, safety, food, and access.

Clustering brought them together —
and surfaced something unexpected.

### What we found challenged our assumptions:

NOT: Lack of infrastructure
BUT: Infrastructure exists — delivery is inconsistent

NOT: Users accept discomfort
BUT: Users accept rawness — not poor service

NOT: Safety is about availability
BUT: Safety is about visibility

This is not a feature gap.
It is a service delivery problem.

### The Values Mismatch

Providers weren't failing through negligence.
They were making deliberate choices:
- Keeping food basic
- Limiting comfort
- Reducing intervention

Because for them, rawness was the product.

Campers saw it differently.
They were willing to give up luxury —
but not safety or basic dignity.

The provider's idea of authenticity was
landing in the camper's experience as neglect.

Neither side was wrong.
They had different definitions of camping.
And nobody had ever made that explicit.

---

## SECTION 6: Where the Experience Breaks

### Hygiene: a shared responsibility no one owns
Washrooms exist. But no one is clearly
responsible for maintaining them.
Campers expect providers to manage cleanliness.
Providers expect campers to participate.
The result is predictable — inconsistent hygiene.

### Safety: the absence of a fallback
Campers don't feel unsafe because of isolation.
They feel unsafe because there is nothing
to fall back on. No visible support system,
no emergency response structure,
no reassurance if something goes wrong.

### Food: a values gap disguised as a quality issue
Campers ask for local and simple food.
Providers interpret that as minimal effort.
But campers still expect it to be good.
The assumption that "raw = low quality"
exists only on the provider side.

### Accommodation: discomfort without meaning
Basic sleeping setups are intentional.
But not all discomfort adds to the experience.
Campers will endure difficulty —
but not when it feels unnecessary.

Breakdowns don't happen at touchpoints.
They happen in how the experience is
interpreted and delivered.

---

## SECTION 7: Key Insights

### 1. The system fails because responsibility
### is assumed, not defined.
Camping services rely on shared expectations
between campers and providers.
But those expectations are never made explicit.
Each side assumes the other is responsible —
and the system breaks in between.

### 2. Authenticity is being misinterpreted in delivery.
Providers design for rawness.
They intentionally reduce comfort to
preserve the experience.
But what they remove isn't luxury — it's reliability.

### 3. The experience is shaped by the most basic moments.
The biggest drops in satisfaction don't
happen in extreme situations.
They happen during routine interactions:
using the washroom, eating a meal.
These moments are unavoidable —
and they define how the entire trip is remembered.

### 4. Safety is about perception, not presence.
Support systems may exist.
But if users don't see them, they don't feel safe.
In isolated environments,
visibility becomes reassurance.

### 5. Camping is being run as an operation,
### not designed as an experience.
Food is arranged. Transport is managed.
Accommodation is set up.
But no one is responsible for how it
feels when all of it comes together.

## SECTION 8: Nomadology — The Proposed Service

### Izza's Journey With Nomadology
Same Izza. Same campsite.
Completely different experience.

Her emotional curve no longer drops.
Every previous low point has been replaced
by a deliberate, designed touchpoint.

**Pre-camping**
A preliminary health check at a base location.
Allergies and preferences recorded.
A meet and greet with fellow campers before
the journey begins.
Izza leaves prepared — not anxious.

**Travelling**
A treasure hunt activity guides the group
to the campsite.
Refreshment stalls mid-journey.
The travel phase becomes part of the experience.

**Arriving**
Photo-booth signboards communicate what's ahead:
terrain, facilities, and activities.
Izza is oriented and curious before
she's even seen her tent.

**Washrooms**
Pathway lighting at night.
Memes and riddles on walls —
behaviour design shifting camper ownership
through humour rather than instruction.
Toiletry kit provided. Dustbins at every exit.
The shared ownership problem designed
into the physical space.

**Dinner**
Self-cook stations with condiment counters.
Crockery from natural materials.
Izza chooses what she eats and how she seasons it.
The food problem reframed — not by taking
control from providers, but by giving agency to Izza.

**Night**
Campfire mindfulness session — opt-in,
sensory, unhurried.
The serenity she actually came for,
built deliberately into the end of the day.

**Travelling back**
Feedback form.
Cleanliness discount for campers who
maintained their space —
closing the shared ownership loop that
had been absent from the service entirely.

## SECTION 9: Testing the Service

Two rounds of physical testing before
the digital prototype.

### The Essentials Kit
A branded kit given to every camper on arrival
covering hygiene, comfort, and basic safety.

Round 1 findings:
- Candle flagged as fire hazard
- Sanitary napkin better suited to
  a medical vending machine
- Dental tablets and toothbrush together felt redundant
- Travel mirror seen as unnecessary weight

Kit refined based on feedback before moving on.

### The Mindfulness Session
A guided sensory session at end of day —
stars, hot tea, natural scents,
grass underfoot, ambient sounds.

Tested virtually, then physically.

"The tea is comforting. Felt alive.
My hands feel cute."

One concern came up consistently:
what about campers who didn't want to participate?

This shaped the final design: opt-in,
spatially bounded, with a clear perimeter
separating participants from the wider campsite.

### Website Usability Testing
Visual design landed strongly.
Navigation was clear across all users.

Friction points found:
- Black and white booking image read as unavailable
- Amenity information absent from booking page
- Promise-delivery gap between service claims
  and website communication

The usability test closed the research loop:
we started by understanding what was broken
in the existing camping experience, and ended
by testing whether our proposed solution
delivered on its promises.

---

## SECTION 10: Reflection

The brief gave us four problems.
The research gave us one.

That shift — from fixing safety, food, sanitation,
and accommodation as parallel issues to resolving
a values mismatch between two groups who had never
articulated their disagreement — was the most
significant intellectual move of the project.

Following Izza through the research kept us grounded.
Every time a decision felt abstract, we came back
to a simple question:
does this change something real for her?
If the answer was no, the idea was wrong —
regardless of how elegant it seemed.

**What I would do differently:**
More time with providers. The most generative
findings came from the gap between provider intent
and camper experience. We had five providers.
I'd want ten — enough to understand not just
what the philosophy was, but where it came from
and what it would take to shift it.

**What I would do next:**
A live pilot with two or three real camping
providers — specifically testing the operational
interventions in the service blueprint.
Only a real pilot would show whether those
backstage changes are actually viable.
That's the most critical untested question
in the entire design.

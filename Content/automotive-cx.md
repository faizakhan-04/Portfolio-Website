## SECTION 1: Overview

A Dubai-based automotive group was mid-way through a digital
transformation — replacing a manual, fragmented lead management
process with a unified CRM spanning four roles: call centre
agent, sales executive, sales manager, and showroom manager.
I was brought in to design the service blueprint connecting
the customer-facing journey to that new backend system, and
to design the WhatsApp conversational flows for the leads and
customers already in the pipeline.

---

## SECTION 2: The Problem — A Process With No Single Owner

The existing lead-to-sales journey was entirely manual, and it
showed in the customer experience: late responses to new leads,
no unified way to track where a lead actually stood, and long
wait times with no visibility into the cause. Ownership of a
lead moved across four different roles with no shared system
connecting them. The business's answer was a new CRM. My job
wasn't to build the CRM — it was to make sure the customer
experience was designed into it, rather than assumed.

---

## SECTION 3: My Role — Blueprinting the Future State

I mapped the end-to-end future-state journey, from first lead
capture through to sale close, blueprinting two layers at once:
what the customer experiences, and what has to happen behind
the scenes for that experience to hold up. That meant mapping
the customer journey stage by stage, identifying where
notifications and surveys needed to sit in the flow, and
mapping backend handoffs across all four roles so a lead
didn't lose momentum — or visibility — moving between them.
The work was less about inventing a new experience and more
about making sure the new system didn't quietly rebuild the
old one's blind spots in different software.

---

## SECTION 4: WhatsApp — Designing for Leads and Customers Already in the System

A chatbot was already in development for brand-new leads when
I joined. My scope was the three personas that come after that
first contact:

### Existing Lead
Someone who'd already spoken to a sales advisor but hadn't yet
purchased. The core design problem was continuity: the bot
couldn't start from zero without undoing the relationship a
human advisor had already built. I designed the flow to branch
on whether an advisor had made contact and where the person
stood in the test-drive process, so the bot picked up the
conversation where the human left off. Since live advisor
handoff wasn't available in this system, "Speak to an advisor"
became "Request a callback" or "Request a follow-up call," so
the promise matched what the backend could deliver.

### Existing Customer, with and without an active service booking
For customers with no open booking, a simple top-level menu:
book a service, request assistance, or see more options. For
customers with an active booking, status-based branching —
Scheduled, In Progress, Ready for Pickup — so the message and
available actions matched where their car actually was.

### Deliberate cuts
Across both, I made deliberate cuts: Roadside Assistance was
removed once we confirmed it was insurance-handled, not
something the business could act on, and separate complaint
categories were merged into one "Request Assistance" entry
point, since customers don't think in a company's internal
category names when something's gone wrong.

---

## SECTION 5: Reflection

This was a different kind of test than my earlier research-led
work: no room for a multi-week discovery phase, four
stakeholder roles who each had their own definition of "done,"
and a live system being built in parallel. It reinforced
something I keep coming back to — the customer experience
rarely breaks at the visible touchpoint. It breaks in the
handoff no one owns.
